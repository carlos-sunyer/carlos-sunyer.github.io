Working papers

State Income Taxation and the Allocation of Bank Credit Supply

Job Market Paper · 2026

Abstract

This paper documents the importance of the banking system in transmitting personal income tax changes to the real economy. Using U.S. state tax reforms and granular banking data, I show that banks whose deposit base is exposed to state tax increases reduce their credit supply relative to unexposed banks. Consistent with taxpayers drawing down their bank balances to absorb part of the decline in disposable income, tax increases lead to lower deposit supply in the reforming state. Banks exposed to these outflows do not replace the lost deposits with alternative funding. As a result, they respond by cutting small business and mortgage lending, both within and outside the reform state. At the aggregate level, counties more exposed to these banks experience weaker real economic activity. Overall, the effects I document are sizable and highly asymmetric, as tax cuts generate no comparable expansion in deposits or lending.

Political Polarization and Human Capital Allocation (SSRN, CEPR)

with David Martínez-Miera · 2025

Abstract

This paper provides empirical evidence on the negative effects of political polarization on human capital allocation and hospital outcomes. Levering Spain’s centralized rank-based system for allocating recently graduated doctors to hospitals, we obtain (i) a precise measure of each doctor’s quality and (ii) their work-location choices. Exploiting a plausibly exogenous increase in political polarization in a Spanish region, we find that polarization reduces the average quality of incoming doctors, as higher-ranked candidates become less likely to select positions in the affected region. Examining the underlying mechanisms, we find evidence consistent with conflict avoidance rather than ideological sorting. Consistent with the documented decrease in doctors’ quality, we document an increase in risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality rates. Our findings highlight the broader economic and social costs of political polarization, particularly its impact on the distribution of skilled professionals and health.

Brick Booms, Brain Drains

with David Martínez-Miera and Di Zhang · 2026

Abstract

Positive sorting of talent into locations with high house prices can reverse when wages are weakly linked to worker productivity. Using Spain’s centralized, rank-based allocation of medical residents, we estimate the causal effect of house prices on talent allocation. To address endogeneity concerns, we instrument housing demand with a shift-share instrument based on immigration flows. At the individual level, we document that a 10% increase in relative house prices reduces candidates’ probability of choosing a residency position by 18%, with a greater impact on candidates from lower-income backgrounds, consistent with credit constraints limiting spatial mobility. At the hospital level, a 10% increase in relative house prices reduces average incoming talent by about 4%. Our findings underscore the importance of house prices for talent allocation in labor markets with limited wage flexibility.

Attracting Talent to General Practice through Financial Incentives (SSRN)

with Lucía Cobreros and Judit Vall · 2025

Abstract

Shortages of general practitioners (GPs) strain healthcare systems worldwide. We test whether wage incentives can attract higher-ability physicians using a 2022 reform in Catalonia, Spain, that granted incoming GP residents unconditional salary supplements of 26% to 47% of base pay across three tiers. Using administrative data on medical residency choices nationwide and a synthetic difference-in-differences design, we document two effects. First, the reform raised entrants’ standardized exam scores by 0.067 standard deviations on average (0.174 in the highest subsidy tier) and increased the international resident share by nearly 30 percentage points. Second, GP vacancies emerged nationwide after 2022 but rose 4.4 percentage points less in Catalonia, drawing lower-scoring candidates to the newly filled slots and mechanically diluting the average score gain. Lee bounds correcting for this compositional shift raise the estimated effects to 0.088 and 0.191 standard deviations in a fully cleared market. Our findings show that salary supplements can improve the allocation of medical talent to shortage occupations, though their effectiveness depends on local labor-market tightness and physician supply.

Paying for Parity: Incentivizing Men in Feminized Occupations

with Lucía Cobreros and Judit Vall · Economics Letters, 2026

Abstract

We find that gender-neutral financial incentives increased the share of male General Practice (GP) residents by 30% in Catalonia, Spain. Exploiting a salary supplement policy for GP residents and a synthetic difference-in-differences design, we estimate an increase of 8.3 percentage points, with effects concentrated in areas receiving larger wage increases. We also document suggestive evidence of lower GP vacancy rates in treated regions after the policy, although this pattern cannot be interpreted causally. To our knowledge, these results provide the first causal evidence that gender-neutral financial incentives can attract men into a highly feminized occupation.

Technology Catch-up in Agriculture among Advanced Economies

with Yu Sheng, Eldon V. Ball, Kennet Ericson and Carlos San Juan Mesonada · The Australian Journal of Agricultural and Resource Economics, 67(1), 2023

Abstract

We examine whether countries with low initial levels of agricultural total factor productivity (TFP) tend to ‘catch up’ with the technology leaders. We first compare relative levels of agricultural TFP, capital services and labour input levels in agriculture for 17 OECD countries between 1973 and 2011. Then we apply (conditional) convergence analysis to the panel data to examine the speed of convergence and test whether the convergence is transitory or permanent by analysing TFP changes over the business cycle. Capital intensities, quality improvement of capital, factors such as human capital spillovers, and certain agricultural policies are conditioning variables. We examine how differences in relative capital intensities affect agricultural productivity convergence over the business cycle. We find evidence that the speed of convergence increases during periods of contraction in economic activity.
